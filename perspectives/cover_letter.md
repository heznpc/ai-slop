Dear Editors,

I am submitting "Beyond the Video Frame: The Production-Detection Asymmetry in Multi-Format AI Slop" for consideration as a CACM Viewpoints article.

Four days ago, OpenAI shut down Sora. The closure has been widely celebrated as a turning point in the AI slop crisis, but it addresses only one content format -- video -- while the text-based and hybrid-format slop that saturates search results, academic journals, product reviews, and social media carousels grows unchecked. This piece argues that the moment of highest public attention to AI slop is precisely the wrong time to let the field's video-centrism go unchallenged.

CACM's readership -- researchers and practitioners who build and study the systems that produce, distribute, and detect AI-generated content -- is the ideal audience for this argument. The core contribution is the production-detection asymmetry (PDA) framework, which reframes the AI slop problem around the gap between how cheaply each content format can be produced and how reliably it can be detected. The framework reveals a systematic misallocation: the formats with the highest PDA (text, image-text hybrids, product reviews) receive the least detection research, while video, where detection already works best, absorbs the most resources. The piece generates five testable hypotheses to guide reallocation.

I write from the perspective of an independent developer who builds content pipelines and observes the production side of AI slop firsthand. This practitioner vantage point complements the academic detection literature by grounding the argument in the operational realities of automated content production -- the infrastructure that most research studies from the outside.

The manuscript is approximately 2,700 words with one table, one conceptual figure, and 33 references.
