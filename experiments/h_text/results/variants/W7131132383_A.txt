Picture a classroom where every student has a dedicated tutor, where grammar errors draw instant correction, and where a virtual interlocutor stands ready for conversation practice at any hour. This is not a speculative scenario. Artificial intelligence is remaking English language instruction at a pace researchers compare to the internet's arrival in education — except the disruption forecast for the coming decade is expected to run deeper.

A recent synthesis pulling from linguistics, computer science, and education policy charts the trajectory of the field: where it is moving, and where it risks going off course.

The first transformation is already underway: personalization that scales. Where a teacher might not notice a student's persistent trouble with irregular verbs or academic register until weeks have passed, an AI system flags the pattern in real time and reshapes the lesson accordingly. The same infrastructure drives automated essay feedback — tools that return commentary within seconds, addressing not just surface errors but weaknesses in argument structure and tone, the sort of layered critique that historically demanded a trained human reader.

Oral and listening skills, historically the hardest to develop without access to native speakers, are seeing comparable advances. Conversational agents can stage realistic dialogues; immersive virtual environments drop learners into a London café or a corporate negotiation without anyone leaving the building. These tools now deliver genuinely useful practice, researchers acknowledge, though the spontaneous texture of real human exchange remains beyond their reach.

The case for AI in teaching is not that it displaces instructors but that it removes the drudgework. Differentiated worksheets, progress tracking, routine assessment — machines can absorb that labor, returning teachers to mentorship, substantive discussion, and the relational work that no algorithm replicates. Several programs already deploy AI-generated dashboards that surface struggling students before those students quietly stop trying.

The same researchers who document these gains are unsparing about the hazards. Language models built primarily on dominant dialects and formal written registers will systematically disadvantage students whose linguistic backgrounds sit outside that norm, hardwiring existing inequities into automated scoring. The movement of student data through commercial platforms opens privacy questions most institutions are not prepared to confront. A further concern is gathering force: sustained dependence on automated feedback may degrade students' capacity to judge their own writing independently, leaving them reliant on a machine's verdict.

The policy recommendations the analysis produces are notably unglamorous. Schools should integrate AI alongside human instruction, not as a replacement for it. They should require vendors to make their decision logic transparent. They need data-use policies established before platforms are deployed, not after. And they must commit to preparing teachers to work with these tools actively rather than to simply step aside for them. Whether AI ends up leveling language education or merely accelerating its existing disparities, the researchers contend, will come down to whether institutions take that unglamorous work seriously.
