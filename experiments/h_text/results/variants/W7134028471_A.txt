Science fiction's robots — hulking metal bodies, glowing red sensors, arms that grind and whir — bear almost no resemblance to what labs are actually producing now. Drones survey wreckage and flood zones. Pliable, air-filled structures squeeze through rubble. Sensor-laden garments track a wearer's pulse in real time. Materials deform and recover on demand. Conversational AI agents negotiate, influence, and assist without occupying any physical space whatsoever. The field has shifted enough that the word "robot" may have outlived its usefulness.

That problem of definition has moved from the margins into active academic debate. An expanding cohort of researchers and designers maintains that the boundaries of robotics — what qualifies as a robot, what it means to encounter one, and who holds the authority to decide — are not simply widening but splintering in multiple directions at once. These same researchers argue that Human-Computer Interaction, a discipline long organized around displays and input devices, has become, with relatively little fanfare, one of the central venues for resolving those tensions.

HCI carries tools that conventional robotics rarely develops on its own. Rather than asking only whether a system performs correctly, it asks how people *feel* encountering it — the spatial and bodily impressions it generates, the aesthetic register it occupies, the degree to which it integrates into the texture of everyday existence. Concerns once treated as secondary or imprecise are now foundational to any design effort aimed at producing systems people will genuinely accept into their lives.

Researchers met across two workshop sessions to engage this challenge head-on. The objective was not to produce a consensus definition of robotics but to chart the competing definitions in circulation — and to examine what is actually decided when one definition is chosen over another. A drone administering medicine to a remote patient operates within a fundamentally different set of social and ethical conditions than an AI companion conducting conversation, even if both systems could reasonably be classified as robotic. The choice of category carries real consequences, not just conceptual ones.

Those sessions produced a collective push toward what participants named "relational robotics" — an orientation that repositions the human relationship as the primary design problem rather than something to be addressed after the engineering is settled. Within this framing, a robot is characterized not by its hardware or its degree of autonomy but by the nature and quality of how it engages with people.

The research agenda now taking shape pursues questions the field has largely set aside: what kinds of relationships robots ought to make possible, which they should foreclose, and whether the discipline of interaction design — more than mechanical engineering — will prove to be the decisive force in shaping what robots ultimately are.
