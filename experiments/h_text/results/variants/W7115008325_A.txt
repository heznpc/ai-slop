Historic wooden bridges rank among the most visually arresting and culturally resonant forms of traditional Chinese construction — yet preserving them depends on identifying, precisely and early, the locations and mechanisms of structural failure. A newly assembled image dataset for crack detection in heritage timber bridges is designed to make that identification faster and more systematic, both for human engineers and for machine-learning systems.

The data were collected at the Lingyan Wind and Rain Bridge, a covered timber structure of traditional design situated within Gantang River Park, Lingchuan County, Guilin City, in southern China's Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Researchers conducted photographic surveys of the bridge's wooden load-bearing members under ordinary field conditions, deliberately capturing the lighting variation, pronounced grain texture, and visual noise that characterize actual inspection environments rather than controlled settings.

The collection encompasses four crack categories. Split cracks propagate parallel to the grain and typically originate from tensile forces acting along the wood's length. Tearing cracks arise from sudden mechanical loading that severs fibers across a wider cross-section. Linear cracks present as narrow, rectilinear fractures, commonly attributable to drying processes or the slow accumulation of applied load. Shrinkage cracks develop as moisture content decreases and the wood contracts unevenly over time — a deterioration pattern encountered with particular frequency in aging timber.

The dataset's technical value is grounded largely in its annotation scheme. Each image is accompanied by a structured XML file in VOC format, encoding the precise location and type of every crack visible in the photograph. This pairing positions the data for direct use in computer-vision workflows spanning object detection, multi-class classification, pixel-level segmentation, and evaluation of model performance under challenging real-world imagery.

The intended application domain is structural health monitoring — the systematic, ongoing process of determining whether a bridge or building is accumulating damage at a rate that may eventually threaten its integrity. Conventional inspection requires trained engineers to traverse the structure in person and document defects manually, a workflow that is labor-intensive, costly, and sensitive to individual assessor skill. Inspection systems augmented by machine learning and trained on annotated datasets of this kind can flag candidate cracks in imagery automatically, allowing specialist attention to be directed where it is most consequential.

Many wooden bridges in the rural highlands and river valleys of southern China occupy climatic zones that hasten timber deterioration, and a substantial number carry heritage designations that impose constraints on maintenance methods while simultaneously raising the stakes for their preservation. A labeled crack dataset grounded in authentic field photography rather than laboratory-staged imagery represents a tangible contribution toward intelligent monitoring infrastructure capable of operating reliably under the unpredictable conditions of active built environments.
