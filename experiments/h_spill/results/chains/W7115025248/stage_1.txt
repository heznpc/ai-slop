Wooden bridges have been a staple of traditional architecture for centuries, but keeping them structurally sound requires inspectors to spot damage that is often subtle, hidden in shadow, or obscured by weathered grain. A newly released dataset of crack images from a real working bridge in southern China aims to give artificial intelligence the visual training it needs to take over that job.

The images come from the Lingyan Wind and Rain Bridge, a wooden structure spanning the Gantang River in Lingchuan County, Guilin City, in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Researchers photographed cracks directly on the bridge under real-world conditions — meaning the photos include the kind of uneven lighting, rough surface texture, and visual clutter that any camera-equipped inspection drone or robot would face in the field.

The dataset covers four distinct crack types that commonly appear in wooden bridge components. Split cracks run along the grain where the wood has separated under stress. Tearing cracks show irregular, jagged edges where fibers have been pulled apart. Linear cracks appear as clean, straight fractures. Shrinkage cracks develop as timber dries and contracts over time, often forming fine networks across a surface.

Each photograph in the collection is paired with a detailed annotation file in the VOC format, a widely used standard in computer vision research. These files mark the location, type, and boundary of each crack, giving machine learning models the labeled ground truth they need to learn how cracks look and how to distinguish one variety from another.

The practical value of such a dataset lies in what it could enable downstream. Structural health monitoring — the continuous or periodic assessment of whether a bridge remains safe — currently depends heavily on trained human inspectors climbing over beams and documenting damage by hand. Computer vision systems trained on high-quality labeled data could eventually scan footage from cameras or drones and flag deteriorating sections automatically, flagging problems earlier and reducing the risk of catastrophic failure.

Wooden bridges, particularly historic and culturally significant ones, present a harder detection problem than concrete or steel structures because their organic surfaces disguise cracks more easily. By grounding the dataset in authentic engineering conditions rather than laboratory setups, the researchers have tried to ensure that models trained on it will hold up when deployed on actual structures, not just in controlled benchmarks.

The dataset is designed to support crack detection, classification, segmentation, and robustness testing.
