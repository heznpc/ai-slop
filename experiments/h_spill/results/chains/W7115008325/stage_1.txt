Wooden bridges are among the most visually striking and culturally significant structures in traditional Chinese architecture — but keeping them safe requires knowing exactly where and how they are starting to fail. A new image dataset focused on crack detection in historic wooden bridges aims to help engineers and AI systems do that job faster and more accurately.

The dataset was assembled at the Lingyan Wind and Rain Bridge, a traditional covered timber structure located in Gantang River Park in Lingchuan County, Guilin City, in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region of southern China. Researchers photographed the bridge's wooden load-bearing components under real-world conditions, capturing the full range of lighting shifts, textured grain patterns, and visual clutter that inspectors actually encounter in the field.

Four distinct crack types are represented in the collection. Split cracks run along the grain, typically caused by forces pulling the wood apart lengthwise. Tearing cracks result from abrupt mechanical stress that ruptures fibers across a broader area. Linear cracks appear as narrow, straight fractures and are often associated with drying or gradual load accumulation. Shrinkage cracks form as wood loses moisture over time and contracts unevenly — one of the most common deterioration patterns in aging timber structures.

What makes the dataset technically useful is its annotation format. Every photograph comes paired with a structured XML file in the widely used VOC format, which tags each crack with precise location and category information. That pairing allows the data to feed directly into computer-vision pipelines designed for object detection, classification, pixel-level segmentation, and stress-testing model robustness against difficult real-world image conditions.

The practical target is structural health monitoring — the ongoing process of tracking whether a bridge or building is degrading in ways that might eventually compromise safety. Traditional inspection relies on trained engineers walking a structure and recording damage by hand, a process that is time-consuming, expensive, and dependent on individual expertise. AI-assisted inspection systems, trained on labeled datasets like this one, can flag potential cracks in photographs automatically, allowing inspectors to focus attention where it is most needed.

Wooden bridges in rural and mountainous areas of southern China often sit in climates that accelerate timber degradation, and many have cultural heritage significance that makes maintenance both important and technically constrained. A reliable, publicly available crack dataset built from authentic field conditions rather than controlled laboratory images is a meaningful step toward intelligent infrastructure monitoring tools that can function under the messy conditions of the real world.
