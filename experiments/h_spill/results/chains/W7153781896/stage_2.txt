I've been thinking about something that's been bothering me for a while, and a paper I read recently finally put words to it.

We keep talking about AI reliability like it's a binary — either the system works or it doesn't. But the more I watch these systems behave in production, the more I think that framing misses everything interesting. What I find fascinating about this new framework is that it starts from a different premise entirely: the variability isn't a bug. It's structural. Every output is a draw from a probability distribution spread across this abstract space of possible meanings. Once you accept that, a lot of things click into place.

The distinction that hit me hardest is between prompt engineering and what the researchers call substrate engineering. I've always sensed these were categorically different operations, but I couldn't articulate why. Now I can. Prompting moves you around within the existing behavioral territory. Retraining or fine-tuning reshapes the territory itself. The map changes, not just the route. That's a fundamentally different risk profile, and I think most practitioners don't treat it that way.

The Silent Authority Transfer metric is the part I keep coming back to. The idea that a system's decision-making thresholds can drift during a substrate update — quietly, invisibly, while everything looks fine on the surface — is exactly the failure mode I'd lose sleep over. The system passes your evals. The behavior has still shifted. You won't know until something downstream breaks.

The Perturbation-Recovery Profiling test is elegant because it connects empirical measurement to something mathematically rigorous. Recovery time and Lyapunov stability turn out to be the same property. That's not a coincidence — it's a bridge I didn't know I needed.

The composite Trust Index ties it together into something actionable. A single score. How close is the system to its limits? Can it discharge accumulated instability? Are collapse signals detectable early enough to matter?

That last question is the one I'd start with.
