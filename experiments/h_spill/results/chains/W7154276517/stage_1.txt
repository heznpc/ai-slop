When researchers test whether AI systems make ethical judgments, they typically ask a model the same moral question and check whether it gives the right answer. But a new study reveals a flaw in that approach: the same underlying dilemma, rephrased in slightly different words, can produce meaningfully different responses from some models — and standard tests would never catch it.

The research introduces a metric called Moral Consistency Variance, or MCV, designed to measure exactly this kind of instability. Rather than asking whether a model picked the correct option, MCV tracks how much the model's decision-making shifts when a scenario is presented through different phrasings while the core facts and available choices stay identical. The idea is to distinguish genuine moral reasoning from responses that are sensitive to superficial features of how a question is written.

To build the benchmark, researchers created 50 hypothetical ethical dilemmas spanning ten moral themes — topics like resource allocation, harm avoidance, and competing duties. Each scenario was then wrapped in five different prompt formulations, keeping the situation and the options the same but varying the framing and discourse style. A manual check of randomly selected prompt pairs confirmed that the rewrites preserved the underlying facts in all reviewed cases.

Four AI models were put through the benchmark. Two were Grok models from xAI operating in non-reasoning mode; one was Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash accessed through Vertex AI; and one was a smaller open-weight model called SmolLM2-360M-Instruct, included as a control.

The results split cleanly. Both Grok models showed extremely low MCV scores and never switched their final decision based on phrasing alone. Gemini showed moderate instability in its underlying probability distributions — the numbers behind its responses shifted more than Grok's — but it rarely flipped its outright choice between options. The small open-weight model sat in an unusual position: its probability distributions were relatively stable, yet it actually reversed its final decision nearly 30 percent of the time across rephrased versions of the same scenario.

That divergence matters. A model could appear consistent when you only look at its binary choices while its internal confidence distribution is quietly shifting — or vice versa. The authors argue MCV surfaces distributional instability that categorical agreement rates alone would miss.

Statistical tests confirm the model differences are real and not noise artifacts in a benchmark this size. The researchers are careful to note that while the results are consistent with what they call "moral mimicry" — the idea that some models are pattern-matching to surface features rather than reasoning through the ethics — that remains a hypothesis, not a proven mechanism. The benchmark is a tool for detection, not a diagnosis of cause.
