The robots of science fiction — clunky metal humanoids, blinking red eyes, mechanical arms — have almost nothing to do with what researchers are actually building today. Drones patrol disaster zones. Soft, inflatable structures crawl through collapsed buildings. Garments embedded with sensors respond to a wearer's heartbeat. Materials shift shape on command. AI agents negotiate, persuade, and assist without any physical form at all. Something has changed, and the word "robot" may no longer be adequate to describe it.

That definitional crisis is now a live debate in academic research. A growing number of scientists and designers argue that the boundaries of robotics — what counts as a robot, what it means to interact with one, and who gets to draw the line — are not just expanding but fracturing entirely. And they say the field of Human-Computer Interaction, long focused on screens and keyboards, has quietly become one of the most important arenas for working that out.

HCI brings a set of tools that traditional robotics often lacks. It asks not only whether a machine functions correctly, but how a person *experiences* it — the sense of proximity and personal space it creates, the aesthetic impressions it leaves, the way it fits into daily life. These concerns, once considered soft or peripheral, are now central to designing systems that people will actually trust, adopt, and live alongside.

Researchers gathered across two workshop sessions to confront that challenge directly. Their goal was not to settle on a single definition of robotics but to map the competing ones — and to understand what is at stake in choosing between them. A drone that delivers medicine operates under different social and ethical assumptions than a conversational AI companion, even if both might technically qualify as robotic systems. The stakes of categorization are practical, not merely philosophical.

What emerged from those sessions was a push toward what participants called "relational robotics" — a framing that puts the human relationship at the center of the design problem rather than treating it as an afterthought. Under this view, a robot is not defined primarily by its actuators or its autonomy, but by the quality and character of its interactions with people.

The research roadmap they are building aims to answer questions the field has largely avoided: what kinds of relationships should robots enable, which should they discourage, and whether interaction design — not mechanical engineering — will ultimately determine what robots become.
