I've been thinking about robots lately, and I keep coming back to how wrong the mental image is. The one I grew up with — chrome body, blinking eyes, stiff mechanical arms — has almost nothing to do with what's actually being built. A drone surveying a flood zone is a robot. So is a soft inflatable sleeve crawling through rubble. So is the AI on the other end of a customer service chat, negotiating a refund with no body at all. We've been using the same word for sixty years and quietly changed what it means.

What strikes me is that this isn't just a semantic problem — it's a design problem, and nobody's really reckoned with it yet. When the category fractures this badly, the assumptions baked into it fracture too. A robot used to imply a certain kind of relationship: machine over here, human over there, clear boundary between them. That model is gone. A garment that reads your heartbeat and responds is technically robotic. So is a conversational agent that learns your preferences and nudges your decisions. Neither of them fits the old framing, and designing them as if they do is a mistake.

What I find interesting is where the serious thinking is actually happening: not in mechanical engineering labs, but in human-computer interaction research. HCI has always asked the questions that robotics tended to skip — not just does it work, but how does it feel? What does it do to your sense of personal space? Do you trust it? Would you invite it into your home? Those used to be soft questions. Now they're the hard ones.

The framing I keep seeing come up is "relational robotics" — the idea that what defines a robot isn't its actuators or its autonomy, but the character of its relationship with a person. I think that's right. And I think whoever figures out the interaction design will shape what these systems become, far more than whoever builds the hardware.
