Banks and financial institutions face a threat that traditional security systems consistently miss: employees or contractors who exploit their own authorized access to commit fraud, steal data, or sabotage operations. Unlike external hackers who must break in, these insiders already have the keys — making them nearly invisible to conventional defenses.

A new study takes aim at this problem with an AI architecture designed specifically to catch malicious insiders before they do damage. The research team built a detection system that fuses three distinct machine learning techniques into a single pipeline, each addressing a different dimension of the problem.

The first component, called GATv2 (Graph Attention Network version 2), maps the web of relationships between users, devices, and systems in a corporate network. Rather than analyzing each login event in isolation, it learns which connections are normal and which are structurally suspicious — much like how a fraud investigator might notice that a clerk suddenly started accessing accounts outside their usual department.

The second component is an LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) network, a type of AI well-suited to sequences. Authentication logs are inherently sequential — who logged in, from where, at what time, and in what order. The LSTM captures behavioral rhythms over time, flagging users whose patterns shift in ways that suggest something has changed.

The third component addresses one of the hardest problems in insider-threat detection: there simply aren't many examples of malicious behavior to learn from. In the dataset used — roughly 17.8 million authentication events — only 1,514 were anomalous. That is less than 0.009 percent. A standard classifier facing this ratio will often label everything as normal and still score well on paper. To counter this, the researchers deployed a GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) to synthesize realistic fake attack examples, artificially balancing the training data. A technique called Focal Loss further pushed the model to pay attention to the rare cases it would otherwise ignore.

The combined architecture outperformed a standalone bidirectional LSTM baseline on a metric called MCC (Matthews Correlation Coefficient), which is more reliable than the commonly used ROC-AUC score when anomalies are extremely rare. The paper argues that the field should move away from ROC-AUC as a default benchmark in cybersecurity contexts, since it can make a model look effective even when it is nearly useless at catching real threats.

The results suggest that treating insider threat detection as a graph problem — one where relationships and timing matter as much as individual events — produces meaningfully better outcomes in realistic, highly imbalanced conditions.
