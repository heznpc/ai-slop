Classrooms where every student receives a personal tutor, instant feedback on their grammar, and lifelike conversation practice with a virtual partner are no longer science fiction. Artificial intelligence is rapidly reshaping how English is taught and learned around the world, and researchers say the changes coming over the next decade will be more profound than anything seen since the introduction of the internet in education.

A new analysis drawing on research across linguistics, computer science, and education policy maps out where the field is heading — and where it could go wrong.

The most immediate shift is personalization at scale. AI systems can now track how individual students struggle with, say, irregular verbs or academic writing conventions, and adjust the lesson in real time rather than waiting for a teacher to notice weeks later. The same technology powers automated feedback tools that can assess a student essay in seconds, flagging not just spelling errors but issues with argument structure and register — the kind of nuanced commentary that once required a trained instructor.

Speaking and listening, long the hardest skills to practice outside a native-speaker environment, are also getting a major upgrade. Conversational AI agents can simulate realistic dialogues, and immersive virtual environments let learners practice ordering coffee in London or negotiating in a business meeting without leaving their school. Researchers note that these tools are becoming good enough to provide meaningful practice, though they still fall short of the unpredictability of genuine human conversation.

For teachers, the promise is not replacement but relief. AI can handle the grind of differentiated worksheet creation, data tracking, and routine assessment, freeing instructors to focus on mentorship, critical discussion, and the relational dimensions of teaching that machines cannot replicate. Several programs are already using AI-generated analytics dashboards to help teachers spot which students are falling behind before they give up.

But the researchers are direct about the risks. Language models trained predominantly on certain dialects and written registers can penalize students from non-dominant linguistic backgrounds, effectively baking existing inequalities into the grading system. Student data flowing through commercial AI platforms raises serious privacy questions that most schools are not yet equipped to answer. And there is growing concern that over-reliance on automated feedback could erode students' ability to evaluate their own writing without a machine's verdict.

The practical recommendations that emerge from the analysis are deliberately unsexy: schools should blend AI tools with human instruction rather than substituting one for the other, demand that vendors explain how their systems make decisions, build clear data-use policies before rolling out new platforms, and invest heavily in training teachers to work alongside these tools rather than around them. Getting that balance right, the researchers argue, will determine whether AI becomes the great equalizer of language education or simply a faster way to reproduce the same old gaps.
