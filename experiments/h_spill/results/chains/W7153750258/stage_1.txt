Researchers have developed a new mathematical framework for predicting when artificial intelligence systems silently stop being trustworthy — and for measuring how quickly they recover when they do.

The work, which itself emerged from an unusual collaboration between multiple AI systems guided by human researchers, addresses a problem that has grown more urgent as AI is embedded in high-stakes decisions: systems can degrade in ways that are invisible until something goes wrong.

At the center of the framework is a geometric model of how an AI thinks. Rather than treating the unpredictability of large language models as a technical imperfection to be engineered away, the researchers argue it is a fundamental characteristic of the systems — something to be measured and managed rather than eliminated. They represent the full range of outputs any AI can produce as a kind of landscape, with valleys corresponding to stable behavioral modes and peaks corresponding to boundaries between different types of responses.

This geometry allows the researchers to draw a sharp distinction between two very different kinds of intervention. Adjusting the prompts or instructions you give an AI — what the field calls prompt engineering — is, in their terms, a small push within an existing valley. The system is nudged but remains recognizably itself. Modifying the underlying model through retraining or architectural changes is something fundamentally different: it reshapes the landscape itself, potentially moving the valleys without any obvious external signal that this has happened.

That second type of change is where the framework introduces its most novel measurement tool, which the researchers call the Silent Authority Transfer metric. When a model is updated, the boundaries that define where the AI draws lines — on sensitive topics, on what counts as a confident answer, on when to defer — can shift without any announcement. The metric is designed to detect and quantify that drift, treating quiet boundary movement as a safety-relevant event in its own right.

Complementing this is a protocol for stress-testing AI systems by measuring how they respond to deliberate disruptions and how long recovery takes. The researchers prove mathematically that recovery time and a classical engineering measure of system stability — the Lyapunov exponent, borrowed from dynamical systems theory — are measuring the same underlying property by two different routes.

The full framework is packaged as a composite Trust Index that incorporates how close a system is to a failure threshold, its capacity to process and clear disruptions, and early warning signals detectable before collapse occurs. The researchers position it as a practical foundation for AI safety evaluation that regulators and developers could apply to deployed systems.
