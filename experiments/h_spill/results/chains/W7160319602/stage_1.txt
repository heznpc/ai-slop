Doctors have long known that catching a disease early can mean the difference between a straightforward treatment and a years-long ordeal. Now, a growing body of research is pushing that premise into new territory — using artificial intelligence to combine multiple streams of patient data at once, rather than relying on any single test or scan.

Most of today's clinical prediction tools are built around one type of information. An imaging model looks at MRI scans. A lab algorithm watches blood markers. A genomics tool scans DNA. Each approach has real value, but each also has a blind spot — because disease does not confine itself to a single signal.

Human health generates data from many directions simultaneously: electronic health records that log diagnoses and prescriptions over years, medical images that reveal structural changes in tissue, wearable devices that capture heart rate, sleep, and activity around the clock, genetic sequences that encode inherited risk, and clinical notes that contain the kind of nuanced observations a physician writes down but no spreadsheet easily captures. The argument, increasingly backed by evidence, is that a model trained to see all of these at once can catch patterns that any single-source model would miss entirely.

Researchers surveying this field have catalogued the range of techniques being developed to fuse these different data streams — a technical challenge comparable, in some ways, to translating between languages that share almost no grammar. The core problem is that a genomic profile looks nothing like a chest X-ray, which looks nothing like a time-series of step counts. Getting a neural network to reason coherently across all of them requires careful architectural choices about when and how to combine the streams.

The survey maps out the main strategies researchers are currently using and identifies where the field still runs into trouble. Missing data is a persistent issue: not every patient has a wearable device, not every hospital has sequenced its patient population, and a model that breaks down when an input is absent is not ready for clinical use. Interpretability — being able to explain why a model flagged a particular patient — remains a significant barrier to physician trust and regulatory approval. Bias is another concern, since models trained predominantly on certain demographic groups can underperform on others.

The researchers also propose a reference architecture intended to give future teams a principled starting point, along with a set of evaluation standards designed to make results comparable across different studies and settings. The goal is not just smarter prediction, but prediction that is reliable, explainable, and fair enough to be trusted at the bedside.
